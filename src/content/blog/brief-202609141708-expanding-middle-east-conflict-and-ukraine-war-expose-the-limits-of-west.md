<p>The shifting dynamics of modern warfare are challenging long-held assumptions of Western military dominance. Historically, the United States operated under the assumption that it could intervene globally with minimal effective pushback, as seen in the conflicts in Iraq and Afghanistan. However, the current escalation involving Iran, Yemen, and Saudi Arabia marks a significant departure, where adversaries are increasingly capable of fighting back. This evolution is driven by the proliferation of drone technology and precision-guided missiles, which alters the traditional calculus of military power and forces major powers to reconsider their strategic options.</p><p>In an interview on the channel Daniel Davis / Deep Dive, conducted by presenter Daniel Davis, professor of politics Richard Sakwa discussed these shifting dynamics. According to Richard Sakwa, the United States has bitten off far more than it can chew in its confrontation with Iran. The professor emphasized that Iran, with its deep historical resilience and a robust network of regional alliances—including the Houthis in Yemen and Hezbollah in Lebanon—cannot be easily coerced. Moreover, Iran's integration into broader Eurasian frameworks, such as the Shanghai Cooperation Organization, and its ongoing economic relations with China provide it with critical diplomatic and economic buffers against Western pressure.</p><p>The economic consequences of this expanding conflict are already being felt globally, particularly in the energy markets. Recent precision strikes by the Houthi movement on key Saudi Arabian energy infrastructure, including a major pipeline bypass, have successfully knocked millions of barrels of oil per day off the market. According to Richard Sakwa, these developments have driven oil prices up, creating a polycrisis that directly impacts the cost of living in both the United States and Europe. The professor noted that the pinpoint accuracy of these attacks demonstrates how the technological playing field has leveled, leaving traditional security guarantors unable to protect vital shipping lanes and energy hubs.</p><p>This inability to secure regional assets is forcing historic shifts in West Asian diplomacy. With Saudi Arabia unable to halt incoming strikes and the United States refusing or unable to provide unlimited defense systems, regional players are looking for alternative arrangements. Richard Sakwa highlighted the signing of the Mecca Accords between Saudi Arabia, Pakistan, and Turkey as an indicator of a post-Western security alignment. This emerging security architecture, which other nations like Bahrain and Bangladesh are considering joining, suggests that regional powers are taking matters into their own hands, further eroding Washington's influence in the region.</p><p>Turning to Eastern Europe, the conversation addressed the stagnation of the Russia-Ukraine war and the mounting domestic pressures within Ukraine. In the interview conducted by Daniel Davis, Richard Sakwa analyzed recent remarks by Donald Trump regarding Ukraine's strikes on Russian oil refineries. While Donald Trump claimed that an agreement was reached to stop these attacks to stabilize global diesel prices, Richard Sakwa expressed skepticism that Russia would agree to such terms without significant concessions, given that its winter strategy is aimed at dismantling Ukraine's remaining energy grid. The professor warned that Ukraine is entering an increasingly precarious phase, characterized by severe energy shortages, financial distress, and growing internal political instability.</p><p>Furthermore, the internal political landscape in Kyiv is becoming highly volatile as the military momentum tilts in Russia's favor. Richard Sakwa pointed out that President Volodymyr Zelensky faces immense pressure from radical nationalist factions, such as the battle-hardened Azov elements now organized under the 3rd Army Corps. According to the professor, there is a strategic double-play occurring where these forces are being committed to brutal battles in the Donbas not only to hold the line but also to weaken them militarily, preventing them from destabilizing Kyiv's domestic politics in the event of future peace negotiations. This complex interplay of military attrition and political survival underscores the unsustainable nature of the current conflict, suggesting that a definitive turning point may be reached after the coming winter.</p>
