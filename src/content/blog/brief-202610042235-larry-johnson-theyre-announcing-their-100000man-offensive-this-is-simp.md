<p>Former CIA analyst and counterterrorism expert Lawrence A. Johnson dismantled Saudi Arabia’s announced 100,000-troop offensive in Yemen as theatrical fiction with no operational grounding. He insisted that no competent military command would publicly telegraph such a massive operation months in advance — especially not across terrain where the Houthis control elevation, supply lines, and local intelligence.</p>
<p>Johnson anchored his dismissal in immutable historical precedent: Stalingrad was won by concealment and attrition, Bagration by deception and simultaneity, D-Day by layered misdirection — not press releases. He stressed that announcing troop numbers, timelines, and objectives before mobilization is not strategy but surrender to the enemy’s reconnaissance cycle.</p>
<p>The interview was broadcast by Dialogue Works, hosted by Nima Alkhorshid, in April 2024. Lawrence A. Johnson served over two decades at the Central Intelligence Agency, specializing in Middle East counterterrorism and asymmetric warfare analysis, and later advised U.S. Special Operations Command on irregular conflict frameworks.</p>
<p>He noted that Saudi Arabia has failed to capture even a single major Houthi-held town since 2015 despite repeated “decisive offensives” announced with fanfare. Each declared campaign collapses within weeks — not from battlefield defeat alone, but from logistical paralysis, desertion, and drone-strike attrition that no public relations team can obscure.</p>
<p>Johnson pointed to the Marib front as definitive proof: Saudi-backed forces spent three years massing there, leaked maps and force estimates widely, and still could not breach Houthi outer defenses. The Houthis, he observed, treat such announcements as targeting briefings — adjusting air defense placements, mining approach routes, and pre-positioning anti-tank guided missiles along predicted axes.</p>
<p>He rejected the notion that this reflects Saudi incompetence alone. Rather, it reveals a structural shift: Gulf militaries now prioritize signaling to Western capitals over battlefield efficacy. Troop numbers are inflated for budget negotiations, timelines stretched for diplomatic cover, and victories declared before boots hit the ground — all calibrated for Riyadh’s quarterly security briefings in Washington and London.</p>
<p>Johnson emphasized that Yemen’s topography renders massed conventional assaults obsolete. Wadis, escarpments, and cave networks nullify armor columns and fixed-wing air support — advantages the coalition cannot forfeit without admitting strategic irrelevance. The Houthis do not need to win every engagement; they only need to make every advance costlier than the political capital it generates.</p>
<p>He dismissed Western media coverage that repeats Saudi claims without cross-checking satellite imagery, casualty reports, or frontline testimony as complicit in the illusion. When Reuters cites “Saudi sources” on troop movements while ignoring Sana’a-based field hospitals overflowing with coalition wounded, journalism becomes logistics for empire.</p>
<p>Johnson concluded that the 100,000-troop narrative serves one purpose only: to pressure Ansar Allah into negotiations from a position of perceived weakness. But the Houthis have absorbed this tactic for nine years — and their response remains unchanged: tighten the siege on Taiz, escalate Red Sea operations, and wait for the next announcement to evaporate like mist over the Sarawat Mountains.</p>
