<p>Constitutional lawyer Robert Barnes delivered a searing indictment of the United States’ detention of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro, calling it an illegal seizure of a sitting head of state on sovereign soil. He declared that holding Maduro in a New York federal detention center violates not only the U.S. Constitution but the foundational principles of international law.</p>
<p>Barnes stressed that no U.S. statute authorizes extraterritorial arrest and prosecution of foreign heads of state for conduct occurring entirely outside American jurisdiction. He emphasized that sovereignty is not conditional — it does not vanish because Washington disapproves of electoral outcomes or policy choices.</p>
<p>The interview was broadcast by Dialogue Works, hosted by journalist 'Foreign' (real name SPK_1), on September 26, 2026. Robert Barnes is a constitutional attorney with decades of litigation experience before federal courts and the U.S. Supreme Court, widely cited for his work defending due process rights in politically charged prosecutions.</p>
<p>He traced the legal chain from the initial warrant — issued without Maduro’s presence, without extradition proceedings, and without any prior diplomatic notification to Caracas. Barnes noted that the indictment itself rests on charges drafted under statutes never intended to reach foreign electoral administration or domestic anti-corruption enforcement.</p>
<p>Under U.S. law, he explained, sovereign immunity applies categorically to sitting heads of state unless waived — and Venezuela has not waived it. He added that even if immunity were somehow pierced, the venue, jurisdiction, and evidentiary standards remain fatally defective.</p>
<p>International law, Barnes asserted, treats the unlawful detention of a head of state as an act of aggression — not a prosecutorial step. He cited the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations and the UN General Assembly’s 2005 resolution affirming the inviolability of elected leaders during official travel.</p>
<p>Domestically, he argued, the detention violates the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of a speedy trial — since no trial date has been set, no bail hearing granted, and no indictment unsealed publicly. He pointed out that Maduro has now spent over eleven months in pretrial confinement without charge being formally presented to a jury or judge.</p>
<p>Barnes rejected the notion that “national security” justifies procedural erasure, stating that such claims have historically accompanied every major assault on habeas corpus in U.S. history. He warned that normalizing this precedent invites its immediate replication against other Global South leaders — from Pretoria to Jakarta to La Paz.</p>
<p>He concluded that the case is not about narcotics or corruption — it is about jurisdictional overreach masquerading as law enforcement. And he stated plainly that until Maduro is released, certified as a head of state, and afforded full diplomatic protections, the United States remains in material breach of its treaty obligations and constitutional duties.</p>
