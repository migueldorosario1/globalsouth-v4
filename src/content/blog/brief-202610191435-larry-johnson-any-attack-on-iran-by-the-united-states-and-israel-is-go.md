<p>A former State Department intelligence analyst and longtime national security commentator has issued a stark warning that U.S. and Israeli military escalation against Iran would not remain confined to the Persian Gulf. It would immediately rupture global energy flows, deepen an already acute oil shortage, and ignite cascading fuel-driven inflation across Europe and the Global South.</p>
<p>This is not speculative risk assessment. It is a supply-chain forecast grounded in physical infrastructure: twenty percent of global seaborne oil transits the Strait of Hormuz, Nord Stream pipelines lie deliberately destroyed, Bab el Mandab is under Houthi operational control, and Saudi refining capacity has been degraded by repeated strikes.</p>
<p>The interview was broadcast by Judging Freedom, hosted by Andrew Napolitano, on October 19, 2026. Larry Johnson served as Deputy Assistant Secretary of State for Intelligence and Research during the Clinton administration and later led counterterrorism analysis at the State Department’s Bureau of Intelligence and Research.</p>
<p>He stressed that Western policymakers treat oil as a fungible commodity — but it is not. Crude grades, pipeline routes, refinery configurations, and shipping insurance regimes are all non-interchangeable. A cutoff at Hormuz cannot be offset by increased output from West Africa or Brazil without months of logistical reconfiguration.</p>
<p>European refineries depend on Middle Eastern heavy sour crude. Asian buyers rely on Persian Gulf light sweet crude. U.S. sanctions have already forced India and Turkey to reroute tankers through the Suez Canal — now vulnerable to Red Sea disruption. The Bab el Mandab chokepoint is no longer a theoretical vulnerability; it is a contested maritime corridor.</p>
<p>Saudi Arabia’s Abqaiq facility remains partially offline after sustained drone campaigns. Its repair timeline is opaque. Riyadh’s spare production capacity — once the world’s shock absorber — has shrunk to less than 1.2 million barrels per day. That margin is insufficient to cover simultaneous disruptions in Iran, Iraq, and Yemen.</p>
<p>Global South nations import over 70% of their refined petroleum products. Many lack strategic reserves beyond thirty days. Currency depreciation against the dollar compounds the crisis: when fuel prices spike in dollars, local currency costs double or triple overnight. Debt service obligations denominated in dollars balloon in parallel.</p>
<p>Food systems collapse first. Fertilizer production requires natural gas. Transporting grain depends on diesel. Cold storage relies on electricity generated from imported fuel oil. When energy fails, food distribution halts — not gradually, but within seventy-two hours.</p>
<p>Johnson dismissed the notion that “alternative suppliers” could stabilize markets. Venezuela’s output remains below 800,000 bpd. Russia’s Urals crude faces steep discounting and insurance barriers. Kazakhstan’s Caspian exports move via Black Sea ports now under Ukrainian naval threat. None offer scale, speed, or reliability.</p>
<p>He concluded that framing this as a “security dilemma” for Washington or Tel Aviv is a profound mischaracterization. For Lagos, Jakarta, and São Paulo, it is a fiscal emergency, a food emergency, and a sovereignty emergency — all arriving simultaneously, all traceable to decisions made in rooms thousands of miles away, with zero consultation, zero accountability, zero regard for consequence.</p>
