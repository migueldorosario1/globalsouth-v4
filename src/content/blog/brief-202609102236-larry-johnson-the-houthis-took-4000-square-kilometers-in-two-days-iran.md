<p>The battlefield collapse in Yemen is not a tactical setback but a systemic rupture in the U.S.-Saudi security architecture, according to former U.S. State Department counterterrorism official Larry Johnson. He described the Ansar Allah offensive — seizing roughly 4,000 square kilometers in 48 hours, including Mocha, key Red Sea islands, and the Bab el-Mandeb coastline — as an irreversible strategic reversal.</p>
<p>This rapid territorial consolidation grants Iran direct operational leverage over the world’s most critical maritime energy chokepoint, Johnson argued. Western oil shipments now face credible interdiction unless rerouted through the Suez Canal — a logistical and financial burden that reshapes global energy pricing and insurance markets overnight.</p>
<p>The interview was broadcast by Dialogue Works, hosted by Nima Alkhorshid, on September 10–11. Johnson, a former U.S. State Department counterterrorism official and longtime Middle East analyst based in Moscow, has advised multiple governments on asymmetric conflict and regional power realignments since the early 2000s.</p>
<p>He dismissed U.S. military advisors embedded with Saudi forces as “useless” — not incompetent, but structurally irrelevant in a theater where drone warfare, coastal artillery, and naval denial tactics have rendered conventional air superiority obsolete. Their presence, he said, serves only ceremonial deterrence while failing to prevent coordinated Houthi advances across land, sea, and electromagnetic domains.</p>
<p>Johnson linked the military reversal directly to domestic economic stress inside the United States. Brent crude surged to nearly $108 per barrel, mortgage rates climbed above 7%, and the Strategic Petroleum Reserve hit historic lows — all within days of the offensive’s launch.</p>
<p>He attributed this cascade not to supply shortages alone but to Trump’s renewed pressure campaign against Iran, which he called a self-inflicted economic suffocation. The administration’s doubling down on sanctions and naval posturing triggered panic in commodity markets while accelerating Tehran’s integration into alternative trade corridors.</p>
<p>Regional alliances pledged under the Mecca defense pact — nominally uniting Saudi Arabia, Turkey, and Pakistan — have produced nothing beyond “strongly worded letters,” Johnson observed. No joint command structure exists, no shared early-warning system operates, and no unified doctrine governs responses to Red Sea escalation.</p>
<p>Iran, meanwhile, is now being courted by European capitals and Gulf states alike for political mediation — not as a concession, but as a functional necessity. Its control over Bab el-Mandeb access has shifted it from pariah status to indispensable interlocutor in any maritime security framework.</p>
<p>Johnson concluded that Washington’s coercive toolkit has been hollowed out not by Iranian defiance alone, but by the synchronized withdrawal of Global South actors from dollar-denominated enforcement mechanisms. The Houthis’ battlefield success is less about weaponry than about timing — exploiting a moment when U.S. influence no longer commands automatic compliance, even among its closest regional partners.</p>
